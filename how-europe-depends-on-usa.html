How Does Europe Depend on the U.S. — and Why

From fighter jets and microchips to the gas that heats European homes, the continent's reliance on the United States runs deeper than most defence budgets suggest — and history shows that even close allies have used that leverage before.

Kharytonov Boryslav and Amila KyatovaBRUSSELS —8 min read2026-08-20

At present, a significant share of the key categories of weaponry held by European states is of American origin: up to 46% of the fighter jets in service with European militaries, and 42% of surface-to-air missile systems, are manufactured in the United States. This structure creates a dependency on the United States, since operating modern military systems requires not just an initial purchase but ongoing maintenance, spare parts, ammunition supplies, personnel training and, in some cases, access to related technological systems.

In the event of serious political disagreements with its transatlantic ally, Europe could find itself in a difficult position, discovering that a substantial part of its defence capability is tied to a state outside Europe. Signals pointing toward such an outcome have already appeared since Donald Trump's return to the Oval Office. This raises the question of Europe's ability to respond to international crises independently and effectively, including by military means.

China's geopolitical interests have long extended well beyond its own region, yet one of the central questions in PRC foreign policy remains Taiwan. Its importance to the global economy is hard to overstate: Taiwan accounts for more than 60% of global contract chip-manufacturing revenue and over 90% of the production of the most advanced chips. Europe, whose own share of the global semiconductor market remains comparatively small — around 12.7%, according to a European Commission report — is also dependent on these supply chains. While Europe retains strategically important positions in certain links of the production chain, above all in chip-manufacturing equipment, its dependence on East Asia, and Taiwan above all, in the production of the most advanced semiconductors remains significant. A potential armed conflict over Taiwan would therefore represent not only a foreign-policy crisis for Europe but an economic and technological one as well. This raises the question: does Europe have sufficient independent military capability to defend its own interests in such a crisis without substantial U.S. support? And if not, how much genuinely independent geopolitical decision-making, backed by its own military strength, does Europe actually retain?

The war in Ukraine has also exposed the limits of Europe's existing defence architecture: the fragmentation of national armed forces, shortages in certain categories of weapons and ammunition, and the need for closer coordination on procurement and the development of joint military capabilities. Despite large-scale support for Ukraine from the EU and individual European states, the conflict showed just how important a role the United States continues to play in European security.

At the same time, the war has seriously reshaped Europe's energy structure. Seeking to cut its dependence on Russian gas and support Ukraine, the EU sharply reduced the volume of Russian imports. Between 2021 and 2025, Russian gas supplies to the EU fell from more than 150 billion cubic metres to roughly 36 billion, while over the same period U.S. supplies grew from 18.9 to 75.6 billion cubic metres.

The United States has not replaced Russia as the sole, or even the dominant, supplier: the EU has simultaneously increased imports from Norway, North Africa and other regions, and reduced its overall gas consumption. Even so, the role of the United States in Europe's energy system has grown dramatically. As a result, the relationship between Europe and the U.S. today touches not only security but, increasingly, critical questions of energy as well.

None of this means Europe has simply swapped a Russian dependency for an American one. But concentrating so many defence, technological and energy ties around a single external partner creates political risk — particularly given how much U.S. foreign policy can shift from one administration to the next. A comparable clash between European and American interests has happened before. In the early 1980s, the Reagan administration tried to block European companies from taking part in the construction of a Soviet gas pipeline, extending U.S. export restrictions even to foreign firms that used American technology and licences. European governments pushed back, and after a prolonged transatlantic dispute, Washington was forced to abandon the sanctions.

That historical episode shows that even close alliances do not rule out situations in which economic or technological dependency becomes a tool of political pressure.

What happens if the White House gets another president who decides Europe has been allowed too much?

To understand this dependence in fuller context, it helps to look not only at what Europe was like around the turn of the twentieth century, but at what the United States itself had become.

The U.S. economy changed after the Civil War not only because the North had won and slavery was gradually being rolled back, but through a series of reforms and structural shifts that both North and South underwent in the postwar years. One of the clearest was the spread of a republican model of land ownership from the North to the South, built around a growing class of smallholding proprietors. That model reached the West first and the South later, driven above all by the Homestead Act — through which roughly 270 million acres of land ultimately changed hands — which accelerated the growth of capitalist relations, extended the railroad network, and helped create a free industrial market. Roughly 270 million acres passed through the Homestead Act

During Reconstruction, the South underwent its own redistribution of land, driven above all by wartime devastation and the inability of white planters to hold on to their vast estates. The old model — a single owner presiding over a mass of enslaved labor — had collapsed, and a poorer, weaker postwar Southern economy could no longer sustain plantations on their former scale. Even so, the retreat of slavery did not bring the immediate emancipation that might have marked a genuine turning point in American political and economic history.

Alongside these changes in landholding, Washington passed a series of tariff laws that shielded the postwar American market from European competition — a rivalry that would resurface later. Taken together, the war's economic result was a relatively unified American economy, something the country had not had before, and that unification is what set off the Gilded Age.

Modernization defined the period that followed. American trade increasingly meant exporting manufactured goods while importing raw materials for its factories, marking a shift from a largely agrarian economy to an industrial one. Faster communications and immigration, itself encouraged by the Homestead Act, drove rapid urbanization alongside it.

Politics between the 1870s and the 1890s shaped what came next, because it explains the rise of the monopolies that followed. Politics of the Gilded Age The political system of the era was heavily corrupt and organized around party patronage, with government posts handed out as rewards to supporters rather than assigned on merit — a practice that eventually forced civil-service reform, symbolized by the Pendleton Act of 1883 and its shift toward a merit-based system. That same patronage politics helps explain why the state stayed out of the economy for so long: it built no antitrust apparatus and largely just reinforced the position of large firms by restricting foreign competition. The result was serious market monopolization — by 1890, Standard Oil controlled 88% of American oil refining, and by 1904, 91% of production and 85% of final sales. Before turning to the United States' role in the First World War, it is worth recalling what that war actually was. It marked the culmination of the colonial era — a moment when the world's territorial carve-up was not just finished but, for several powers, deeply unsatisfying. Italy and Germany, fragmented for centuries before their unification, were looking for their own place in the sun and were prepared to go to war for a share of colonial wealth. Europe's older empires were no less committed to that same contest; the difference was that they were fighting to keep what they already held, rather than to win something new.

The preconditions for two rival blocs began forming as early as 1871, when France's unconditional defeat in the Franco-Prussian War cost it Alsace-Lorraine — territory that became the first of a long chain of grievances between the powers.

The next fault line was one of history's least durable alliances: the League of the Three Emperors, linking the German Empire, Austria-Hungary and Russia, meant to keep Russia and France apart. Its natural weak point was the Balkans, which all three powers had designs on — and the Balkans are exactly what broke the League apart. The Congress of Berlin in 1878 pushed Russia away from Austria and drew Germany and Austria-Hungary closer, formalized as the Dual Alliance of 1879; the crisis of 1885–1886 then ended any remaining hope of holding the League together. Even the Reinsurance Treaty between Russia and Germany couldn't keep the two empires aligned — they drifted apart, Germany moved closer to Austria-Hungary, and by the early 1890s Russia and Germany were locked in a tariff war. Around the same time, Germany and the Ottoman Empire began discussing the Berlin–Baghdad railway, a project that would trouble all three future Entente powers, not Russia alone.

In 1882, the Dual Alliance became the Triple Alliance when Italy joined, to the displeasure of Europe's other major powers. In the early 1900s, Germany's naval buildup further alarmed Britain, pushing it to abandon its long-standing policy of Splendid Isolation. France, in response, ended its own diplomatic isolation and formalized the Franco-Russian Alliance between 1891 and 1894. In 1904, France and Britain signed the Entente Cordiale, settling their colonial disputes; in 1907, Britain and Russia did the same, resolving the Great Game and their territorial disputes in the Middle East. With that, the Triple Entente was complete.

By the early 1900s, two militarily minded blocs faced each other across Europe, and the United States belonged to neither. As a result, America avoided the worst of what followed: the devastation of a war that would reshape a continent it had, until then, only traded with and lent money to.
